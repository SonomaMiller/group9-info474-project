Abstract
Climate change has had a massive impact on Washington state over time. With increasing temperatures, and changing weather patterns impacting the amount of rainfall and snow in this state. We will be focusing on rain, snow, and temperature to understand how these three aspects of weather are innately intertwined and can exemplify the impact of climate change on Washington State. By exploring these aspects of climate change, we can identify the broader trends that are driving the day-to-day climate impact on residents in the state.
Story
We have the goal of examining how climate change has affected weather trends in Washington state over time. We will be taking a holistic approach and looking at temperature, precipitation, and snowfall to see how these values have changed and impacted each other over time. In this project, we will explore:
  1. What are past trends in temperature and precipitation?
  2. How has snowpack changed?
  3. How has temperature impacted the amount of rain and snowfall?
Data Sources
We use three different CSV datasets for this project; one mapping snowfall reports for five different locations in Washington, one mapping precipitation in Washington counties, and one mapping temperature in Washington counties. These datasets were accessed through the Washington State Department of Transportation (WSDOT) and the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) websites.
Long-Term Temperature Trends in Washington State

This line chart shows the gradual increase in annual average temperature in Washington State from 1895 to 2026. While year-to-year fluctuations are visible, reflecting natural variability such as cooler and warmer periods, the overall trend is upward. Over the past century, the state has experienced a slow but persistent warming, with recent decades showing consistently higher temperatures compared to the early 20th century.

Climate change is influencing Washington's weather. Even a gradual rise in temperature can have cascading effects on rainfall, snow accumulation, and seasonal weather patterns, affecting daily life, ecosystems, and regional infrastructure.

Dry Season Precipitation

This bar chart displays the total precipitation during Washington State's dry season (July and August) from 1895 to 2026. Unlike the steady upward trend seen in average annual temperatures, dry season rainfall shows high year-to-year variability, with wet summers and near-drought summers alternating throughout the record.

It is important to note that as temperatures have risen, warmer air evaporates moisture more quickly from soils, snowpack, and vegetation, meaning a dry summer today carries greater ecological stress than the same rainfall totals would have a century ago. Washington's forests, rivers, and communities face increasing pressure from a warming baseline that a variable dry season can no longer reliably offset.

Sea Level Over Time

This graph displays the change in mean sea level each year from the years 1899 to 2026. From a top level view it shows that while the sea level is very dynamic, it has steadily been rising since around the 1930s.

Viewed on its own this graph isn't too concerning, the sea level goes from being slightly beneath 0 at -0.2 meters, to being between 0-0.1 meters. However, with the context that mainly places in Washington are built at or below sea level, including major cities such as Seattle and Olympia, any increase is concnerning due to the risk of flooding in populated areas. Additionally, with changing trends in precipitation this puts the state at greater risk and worry for mass flooding, of which we've seen this year.

Steven's Pass Snow Pack over Time

Here we can see the change in snow pack dynamically shown on a mountain over time. This is representative of the snowpack seen on Steven's Pass from 2004-2024. In addition, there is an added filter to show whether the year is El Nino or La Nina, which can have a great impact on the amount of snowpack in a given year.

This is a dynamic way to show the change in snowpack on a year-to-year basis, while also providing the context of El Nino vs La Nina to explain the sometimes drastic changes in snow pack year-to-year. Even there there are large changes in snow pack, we can see that as the years go on, there is a trend of having less snow pack on a yearly basis, which can affect the rest of Washington throughout the year.

Putting it All Together: Heatmap
Here we can see the relationship between temperature and precipitation over time. As temperatures have increased, there has been a decrease in snowfall and an increase in rainfall. This suggests that warmer temperatures are leading to more precipitation falling as rain rather than snow, which is consistent with the observed decline in snowpack. These three aspects of climate change are interconnected and have evolved together over time in Washington State.
